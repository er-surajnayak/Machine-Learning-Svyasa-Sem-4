Bias–Variance
Tradeoff

The two fundamental sources of error in any machine learning model — and why reducing one tends to increase the other. Understanding this tradeoff is essential to every modelling decision you make.

01 · First source of error

What is Bias?

Bias is systematic error — the error that comes from wrong assumptions baked into your model. A biased model consistently misses the target in the same direction, no matter how much training data you give it.

Analogy — The broken compass: Imagine a compass that always points 15° east of true north. No matter where you are, no matter how long you wait — it's always wrong by the same amount in the same direction. That's high bias. The model has a systematic flaw in how it sees the world. More data won't fix a broken compass.

Measuring bias mathematically

Bias measures how far the average prediction is from the true value across many different training sets:

Bias²[E(ŷ) − y]² — squared difference between mean prediction and truth
High bias meansAverage prediction is always wrong in the same way
Low bias meansAverage prediction is close to the true value
02 · Second source of error

What is Variance?

Variance is sensitivity to noise — the error that comes from a model being too sensitive to small fluctuations in the training data. A high-variance model produces wildly different predictions when trained on slightly different datasets.

Analogy — The nervous archer: An archer with high variance is technically skilled but extremely sensitive to the wind, the crowd noise, and their mood. On a calm day they hit the bullseye. On a slightly windy day they scatter shots all over the target. Same skill, wildly different results depending on conditions. That's high variance — your model's predictions change dramatically with different training data.

04 · The tension

Why they trade off

Here's the frustrating truth: you can't minimize both at the same time using the same model family. Reducing bias tends to increase variance, and vice versa. This is the fundamental tension at the heart of machine learning.

Analogy — Memorising vs Understanding: A student who memorises every example (complex model, low bias) can answer known questions perfectly but struggles with anything new — high variance. A student who only learns the broadest summary (simple model, low variance) misses nuance — high bias. The perfect student finds the level of detail that generalises best — the sweet spot.

The sweet spot — minimise total error

The optimal model minimises total test error = Bias² + Variance + Irreducible noise. The sweet spot is where this sum is lowest — neither too simple nor too complex.

Too simple (left)High bias dominates total error
Too complex (right)High variance dominates total error
Sweet spot (middle)Bias² + Variance at minimum — best generalisation
05 · The maths

Error decomposition

The expected generalisation error of any model can be mathematically decomposed into three parts. This is one of the most important equations in all of machine learning theory.

Expected Error = Bias² + Variance + Irreducible Noise

where: Bias² = [E(ŷ) − y]² — systematic error Variance = E[(ŷ − E(ŷ))²] — sensitivity to data Noise = σ² — unavoidable, inherent in data
Bias²

How wrong the model is on average.

Reducible?Yes — use better model
FixMore complex model, more features
AnalogyBroken compass offset
Variance

How inconsistent predictions are across datasets.

Reducible?Yes — regularise, more data
FixSimpler model, regularisation
AnalogyNervous archer scatter
Irreducible Noise

Random noise in the data itself — labels wrong, measurement errors.

Reducible?No — fundamental limit
FixBetter data collection only
AnalogyWind that can't be controlled
Key insight: You can never achieve zero test error — irreducible noise sets a floor. Your job is to minimise the reducible parts (Bias² + Variance) as much as possible. Any remaining error beyond that floor is the noise in the data itself.

07 · In practice

Real-world examples by algorithm

Different algorithms sit at different points on the bias-variance spectrum by default. Knowing this helps you pick the right starting point.

High bias, Low variance

Linear / Logistic Regression makes strong assumptions — that the relationship is linear. This means it'll be wrong in a consistent, systematic way on non-linear data (high bias) but its predictions won't vary wildly between datasets (low variance).

Default positionHigh bias
Reduce bias byAdding polynomial features, interaction terms
Best forLinearly separable data, interpretability
Low bias, High variance

Deep Decision Trees (no max_depth) have very low bias — they can approximate almost any function. But they have extremely high variance — a small change in training data produces a completely different tree.

Default positionHigh variance
Reduce variance byPruning, max_depth, min_samples_leaf
Best fixUse Random Forest (ensemble reduces variance)
Depends on K

K-Nearest Neighbors is a classic demonstration of the tradeoff — K directly controls the bias-variance balance.

K = 1Low bias, very high variance
K = N (all points)Very high bias, zero variance
Optimal KFound via cross-validation
IntuitionLarger K = smoother boundary = lower variance
Low bias, Low variance

Random Forest is an ensemble of many high-variance decision trees. Averaging their predictions cancels out their individual variance while keeping their low bias. This is the core idea behind ensemble methods.

Individual treeLow bias, high variance
After averaging N treesLow bias, low variance ✓
Why it worksVariance(avg) = Variance/N for uncorrelated trees

09 · What to do with it

Practical implications

Understanding bias and variance helps you diagnose a struggling model and choose the right fix — rather than guessing.

?
My model has high training AND test error
›
Diagnosis: High bias (underfitting)

The model is too simple. Try:
→ Use a more powerful algorithm
→ Add more features or polynomial features
→ Reduce regularisation strength (lower lambda)
→ Train for longer / more iterations

Do NOT add more training data as the primary fix — it won't help much when bias is the dominant error.
?
My training error is low but test error is high
›
Diagnosis: High variance (overfitting)

The model is too complex. Try:
→ Get more training data (most effective)
→ Add regularisation (L1, L2, dropout)
→ Use a simpler model or reduce parameters
→ Use ensemble methods (Random Forest)
→ Apply early stopping or cross-validation

Do NOT add more features — it will make variance worse.
?
My model isn't improving despite more data
›
Diagnosis: Likely high bias — more data can't fix it

When bias is the dominant error, adding more training data has diminishing returns. The learning curves (train and test error) plateau early and never converge to a good error level.

More data helps primarily with variance. For bias, you need a fundamentally more expressive model.
Decision guide — what error are you seeing?
High train + high test errorHigh bias → more complex model
Low train + high test errorHigh variance → regularise or more data
Both low but won't improveNear optimal — focus on data quality
High train + low test errorImpossible — recheck your splits
10 · Check your understanding

Quick quiz

Four questions — test yourself on the bias–variance tradeoff.

1. A model has high training error AND high test error. This is a sign of:
A. High bias — model is too simple ✓
B. High variance — model memorised training data
C. Good generalisation — both errors are consistent
D. Irreducible noise — cannot be fixed
High training error + high test error = both bad = high bias (underfitting). The model is too simple to even learn the training data. High variance would show LOW train error but HIGH test error. ✓
2. Which component of the error decomposition cannot be reduced no matter how good your model is?
A. Bias² — always reducible with a better model
B. Variance — reducible with regularisation
C. Irreducible noise — inherent in the data ✓
D. Total error — can always be pushed to zero
Irreducible noise (σ²) comes from randomness and imperfection in the data itself — mislabelled examples, measurement errors, inherent unpredictability. No model, no matter how complex, can eliminate it. It sets the floor for total error. ✓
3. In K-Nearest Neighbors, setting K = 1 results in:
A. High bias, low variance — very smooth boundary
B. Low bias, high variance — memorises every training point ✓
C. Low bias, low variance — optimal performance
D. High bias, high variance — worst case scenario
K=1 means each prediction is based on only one neighbour. This gives zero training error (low bias) but extremely jagged boundaries that change drastically with different data (high variance). Increasing K smooths the boundary, reducing variance but introducing bias. ✓
4. Random Forest achieves low variance compared to a single Decision Tree because:
A. Each tree uses more features than a single tree
B. It applies L2 regularisation to each tree
C. Averaging many uncorrelated trees cancels out individual variance ✓
D. It uses a linear model at each leaf node
Each decision tree has high variance — it's sensitive to its training subset. But when you average N uncorrelated trees, their random errors cancel out. Variance of the average ≈ Variance/N. This is the core principle behind bagging (bootstrap aggregating). ✓
