K-Nearest
Neighbors

The simplest classifier in all of machine learning — no training, just memory. It classifies new points by looking at who their closest neighbours are. Sometimes the most obvious idea is also the most powerful.

01 · The concept

Intuition & analogy

KNN is built on one of the most human ideas imaginable: you are judged by the company you keep. If you want to know what class a new data point belongs to, just look at the K data points closest to it — and take a vote.

Analogy — The new student in school: It's day one of college. You don't know anyone. Where do you sit at lunch? You naturally drift towards people with similar interests. After a week, your "neighbour group" reveals a lot about who you are. A KNN classifier does exactly this — it looks at the K closest training examples and says "you belong to the same group as most of them."
Analogy — House price on a new street: You want to estimate the value of a new house. You don't build a complex formula — you just look at the 5 most similar houses nearby (same size, same area, similar age) and average their prices. That's KNN regression in everyday life.
No training

KNN is a lazy learner. It memorises all training data and does all the work at prediction time — there's no model to train.

Non-parametric

Makes no assumptions about the shape of the data. The decision boundary adapts to whatever pattern exists in the training set.

Instance-based

Every prediction requires comparing the new point to all stored training examples — the training data is the model.

02 · Step by step

How KNN works

KNN has no separate training phase. The entire algorithm runs at prediction time — which makes it beautifully simple and computationally expensive at scale.

Store all training data

Unlike other algorithms, KNN has no training step. You simply store every training example with its label. That's it. The algorithm is "lazy" — it defers all computation until prediction.

Training timeO(1) — just store the data
MemoryO(n) — stores all training points
Compute distances

Given a new query point, calculate the distance from it to every single training example. The most common distance measure is Euclidean distance.

Prediction timeO(n × d) — expensive!
nNumber of training samples
dNumber of features (dimensions)
Find K nearest

Sort all distances and select the K closest training points. These are the "neighbours" that will vote on the class of the new point.

K = 1Only 1 neighbour votes — very noisy
K = 55 neighbours vote — more stable
K = nAll points vote — predicts majority class always
Majority vote

The K neighbours each cast one vote for their class. The class with the most votes wins — that's the prediction. For regression, take the average of their values instead.

Tie-breakingUse odd K for binary, or weight by distance
Weighted KNNCloser neighbours get more voting power
Worked micro-example

New point arrives. K = 3. Find 3 nearest training points:

Neighbour 1 (distance 1.2)Class A
Neighbour 2 (distance 1.8)Class A
Neighbour 3 (distance 2.3)Class B
Vote resultClass A wins 2–1 → Predict Class A
03 · Measuring closeness

Distance metrics

The choice of distance metric is critical — it defines what "nearest" means. Different metrics suit different types of data.

Analogy — Different ways to measure distance in a city: Euclidean distance is as the crow flies — straight line through buildings. Manhattan distance is the actual walking route — you can only move along streets (grid). The right choice depends on whether your "city" has diagonal shortcuts or not.
E
Euclidean Distance (L2)
›
Formula: d = √Σ(xᵢ − yᵢ)²

Intuition: Straight-line "as the crow flies" distance in feature space.

Best for: Continuous numerical features, when dimensions are on the same scale.

Weakness: Sensitive to outliers and to features on different scales — always normalise first.

Default in scikit-learn's KNN.
M
Manhattan Distance (L1)
›
Formula: d = Σ|xᵢ − yᵢ|

Intuition: Grid-walking distance — sum of absolute differences per dimension. Like navigating city blocks.

Best for: High-dimensional data, data with many outliers (more robust than Euclidean).

Why it's robust: Doesn't square the differences, so outliers have less influence.
K
Minkowski Distance (General)
›
Formula: d = (Σ|xᵢ − yᵢ|ᵖ)^(1/p)

Intuition: A generalisation. When p = 1 → Manhattan. When p = 2 → Euclidean. When p → ∞ → Chebyshev (max single-dimension difference).

Best for: When you want to tune the "p" hyperparameter via cross-validation to find the best metric.
Critical: always normalise features before using KNN. If one feature is in the range [0, 1000] and another is [0, 1], the first will completely dominate all distance calculations. Use Min-Max scaling or Z-score standardisation so every feature contributes equally.
# Distance formulas Euclidean: d = √[(x₁−y₁)² + (x₂−y₂)² + ... + (xₙ−yₙ)²] Manhattan: d = |x₁−y₁| + |x₂−y₂| + ... + |xₙ−yₙ| Minkowski: d = (|x₁−y₁|ᵖ + |x₂−y₂|ᵖ + ...)^(1/p)
04 · The critical hyperparameter

Choosing the right K

K is the single most important decision in KNN. It directly controls the bias-variance tradeoff — and there's no "correct" value without testing.

Analogy — Asking for opinions: You want to know if a new restaurant is good. Ask 1 friend → their taste might be totally different from yours (high variance). Ask 100 friends → you get a generic average that ignores your specific preferences (high bias). Ask 7 friends with similar taste → probably just right. K is how many "friends" you consult.

06 · The boundary

Decision boundary & effect of K

The decision boundary of KNN is where the predicted class changes. With small K, the boundary is jagged and complex. With large K, it becomes smoother. The boundary is never explicitly computed — it emerges from the data.

Decision boundary — drag K slider to update
K boundary K = 3
Small K (1–3)

Jagged, complex boundary. Sensitive to every outlier. Overfits. Can perfectly classify training data.

Medium K (5–9)

Smoother boundary. Ignores isolated noise points. Generalises better. This is usually the sweet spot.

Large K (15+)

Very smooth boundary. May miss fine-grained patterns. Approaches predicting the majority class everywhere.

07 · The big weakness

Curse of dimensionality

KNN breaks down in high-dimensional spaces — and it breaks down hard. This is the most important limitation to understand.

Analogy — Finding neighbours in a massive city: In a small village (2D), your 5 nearest neighbours are genuinely close and similar to you. In a massive sprawling city with millions of streets (high-dimensional), your 5 "nearest" neighbours might still be miles away — they share very little in common with you. Distance becomes meaningless when there are too many dimensions.

In high dimensions, all points become approximately equidistant from each other. The difference between the nearest and farthest neighbour shrinks to near zero. When distances lose their meaning, KNN loses its ability to distinguish between similar and dissimilar points.

What happens
2D spaceNeighbours are genuinely close
100D spaceAll distances ≈ similar
1000D spaceKNN essentially random
Data neededGrows exponentially with dims
Fixes
Feature selectionRemove irrelevant features
PCA / reductionProject to lower dimensions first
Feature engineeringKeep only informative features
Different algorithmSVM / tree methods handle high-D better
Volume grows exponentially — an intuition

To cover 10% of the data range in each dimension with nearest neighbours:

1D spaceNeed 10% of the range
2D spaceNeed √0.1 ≈ 32% per side
10D spaceNeed 0.1^(1/10) ≈ 80% per side
ConclusionYour "local" neighbours are no longer local
08 · Beyond classification

KNN for regression

KNN isn't limited to classification. For regression, instead of taking a majority vote, you take the average (or weighted average) of K neighbours' target values.

Analogy — Estimating a house price: You want to price a 3-bedroom house in Indiranagar. You find the 5 most similar houses sold recently (same bedrooms, similar sq ft, same area). Their prices: ₹1.2Cr, ₹1.3Cr, ₹1.1Cr, ₹1.25Cr, ₹1.35Cr. Average = ₹1.24Cr → your estimate. That's KNN regression.

10 · Check your understanding

Quick quiz

Four questions — test yourself on KNN.

1. KNN is called a "lazy learner" because:
A. It uses a very simple algorithm that requires minimal thought
B. It performs poorly on most datasets
C. It defers all computation to prediction time — no training phase ✓
D. It only works on small datasets
KNN is lazy because it doesn't learn a model during training — it just stores all the data. All computation happens at prediction time when it has to search through all training examples. This makes training O(1) but prediction O(n×d). ✓
2. You are using KNN on a dataset with features: Age (0–80) and Income (0–500,000). What is the most important preprocessing step?
A. Remove outliers from the dataset
B. Normalise / scale both features to the same range ✓
C. One-hot encode the features
D. Apply PCA before running KNN
Income has a range of 500,000 while Age has a range of 80. In Euclidean distance, Income will completely dominate — a 1-year age difference is negligible compared to a ₹10,000 income difference. Normalise both to [0,1] or standardise to zero mean/unit variance first. ✓
3. Setting K = 1 in KNN will result in:
A. High bias and low variance — underfitting
B. High bias and high variance — worst case
C. Low bias and high variance — overfitting ✓
D. Low bias and low variance — optimal model
K=1 means each point is its own neighbour, giving 100% training accuracy (low/zero bias). But a single outlier in the training set creates wild boundary changes — predictions are extremely sensitive to the specific training data (very high variance). Classic overfitting signature. ✓
4. Why does KNN perform poorly in high-dimensional spaces?
A. It cannot compute distances in more than 3 dimensions
B. High-dimensional data always has more noise than low-dimensional data
C. All points become approximately equidistant — distance loses meaning ✓
D. KNN can only handle categorical features in high dimensions
The curse of dimensionality: as dimensions increase, the volume of the feature space grows exponentially. Data becomes sparse — all points are roughly the same distance from each other. When nearest and farthest neighbours are equidistant, the "nearest" concept becomes meaningless. ✓
