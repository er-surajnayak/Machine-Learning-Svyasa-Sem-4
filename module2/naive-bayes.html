Naive Bayes
Classifier

A classifier so simple it feels like cheating — and yet it powers most spam filters in the world. Naive Bayes applies Bayes' theorem with one bold simplification and somehow works brilliantly despite it.

01 · The concept

Intuition & analogy

Naive Bayes answers a very direct question: given everything I can see about this data point, which class is most probable? It does this by combining what it knows about how likely each feature is for each class — multiplying probabilities together and picking the winner.

Analogy — The detective's notebook: Detective Bayes has seen thousands of spam emails. She keeps a notebook: "FREE appears in 95% of spam, 5% of legit. WIN appears in 88% of spam, 3% of legit. OFFER appears in 76% of spam, 12% of legit." A new email arrives with all three words. She multiplies the probabilities together and concludes: overwhelmingly spam. She didn't need to read the whole email — she just looked up her notes and multiplied. That's Naive Bayes.
Analogy — The doctor using symptom tables: A patient has fever, cough, and fatigue. The doctor doesn't need a complex decision tree — she checks: "What % of flu patients have fever? Cough? Fatigue? What about cold patients?" She multiplies each probability per disease and picks the most likely diagnosis. Same idea — multiply likelihoods, pick the winner.
Probabilistic

Outputs a probability for each class, not just a label. You know how confident the prediction is.

Fast & Scalable

Training is just counting. Prediction is multiplying. Works on massive datasets with thousands of features — like text.

Surprisingly good

Despite a simplification that's almost never true in practice, it outperforms far more complex models on text classification tasks.

02 · The foundation

Bayes' theorem

Naive Bayes is built entirely on Bayes' theorem — one of the most important equations in probability theory. It lets us update our beliefs based on evidence.

Analogy — Updating suspicion: You suspect your flatmate ate your lunch. Prior probability: 50% (you have two flatmates). Then you find hot sauce on the table — your flatmate loves hot sauce, the other doesn't. Posterior probability: now 80%. You updated your belief using new evidence. Bayes' theorem is the formal way to do this updating.
P(class | features) = P(features | class) × P(class) / P(features)

Posterior = Likelihood × Prior / Evidence

where: P(class | features) — probability of class GIVEN the features (what we want) P(features | class) — how likely are these features if class is true? (likelihood) P(class) — how common is this class overall? (prior) P(features) — how common are these features? (evidence — same for all classes, ignored)
Prior — P(class)

Your initial belief before seeing any features. Estimated from training data — what fraction of training emails are spam?

Example60% of training emails are spam → P(spam) = 0.6
IntuitionBase rate — how common is each class?
Likelihood — P(features | class)

How probable are these specific features given each class? Estimated by counting occurrences in training data.

ExampleP("FREE" | spam) = 0.95
IntuitionHow strongly does this feature point to the class?
Classification rule — ignore P(features)

Since we're comparing classes (spam vs not spam), P(features) is the same for both and cancels out. We just pick the class with the highest numerator:

Predictargmax_class [ P(class) × P(features | class) ]
WinnerWhichever class gives the biggest product
03 · The bold simplification

The "naive" assumption

Here's the trick — and the reason it's called naive. Computing P(features | class) for all combinations of features is computationally impossible. So Naive Bayes makes one massive simplification:

The naive assumption: all features are conditionally independent given the class.

In other words: knowing that "FREE" appears in an email tells you nothing about whether "WIN" also appears — given that the email is spam. Of course this is almost never true in reality — words in a sentence are highly correlated. But making this assumption lets us multiply individual probabilities instead of computing massive joint probabilities.
P(features | class) = P(f₁ | class) × P(f₂ | class) × P(f₃ | class) × ...

Full formula becomes: P(class | f₁,f₂,...,fₙ) ∝ P(class) × Π P(fᵢ | class)
Without naive assumption
Need to computeP("FREE","WIN","OFFER" | spam)
CombinationsExponential — 2ⁿ possibilities
Training data neededAstronomically large
FeasibilityPractically impossible
With naive assumption
Compute separatelyP("FREE"|spam) × P("WIN"|spam)...
CombinationsLinear — n probabilities
Training data neededVery small — just count
FeasibilityTrivially fast ✓
Why does it work despite the wrong assumption? Even though features are correlated, Naive Bayes often still ranks classes in the correct order — which is all you need for classification. The absolute probabilities are wrong, but the relative ordering is often correct. This is why Naive Bayes is surprisingly competitive despite its fundamental flaw.

05 · Variants

Types of Naive Bayes

The difference between Naive Bayes variants is how they model P(feature | class) — the likelihood. Different data types need different probability distributions.

M
Multinomial Naive Bayes
›
Best for: Text classification — word counts or term frequencies

Models: P(word | class) = (count of word in class docs + 1) / (total words in class + vocab size)

Input: Count vectors or TF-IDF vectors — how many times each word appears

Classic use: Spam detection, sentiment analysis, topic classification

sklearn: MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0)
G
Gaussian Naive Bayes
›
Best for: Continuous numerical features — measurements, sensor data

Models: Assumes features follow a Gaussian (normal) distribution per class. Estimates mean μ and variance σ² for each feature in each class from training data.

Input: Real-valued feature vectors

Classic use: Medical diagnosis (blood pressure, glucose levels), iris flower classification

sklearn: GaussianNB()
B
Bernoulli Naive Bayes
›
Best for: Binary features — word present/absent (not count)

Models: P(feature | class) as a Bernoulli distribution — each feature is either 1 (present) or 0 (absent). Explicitly penalises absent features too.

Input: Binary vectors — does this word appear in the document? (yes/no)

Difference from Multinomial: Multinomial uses word count; Bernoulli uses only presence/absence and also factors in words that are missing

sklearn: BernoulliNB(alpha=1.0)
Quick selection guide
Text with word counts / TF-IDFMultinomial NB
Continuous measurementsGaussian NB
Binary presence/absence featuresBernoulli NB
Don't know which to pickTry Multinomial first — most versatile
06 · Fixing the zero problem

Laplace smoothing

There's a critical bug in basic Naive Bayes: if a word never appeared in spam emails during training, P(word | spam) = 0. Multiplying by zero destroys the entire probability product — a single unseen word makes that class score exactly zero, no matter how spam-like everything else looks.

The zero frequency problem: The word "cryptocurrency" never appeared in your training spam. A new email says "FREE cryptocurrency WIN." Because P("cryptocurrency" | spam) = 0, the entire spam score → 0, no matter how strong FREE and WIN are. The model incorrectly classifies it as legit. One zero kills everything.
Laplace smoothing (add-one smoothing): Add 1 to every word count before computing probabilities. This ensures no probability is ever exactly zero — even words never seen in training get a small nonzero probability. Alpha (α) controls the smoothing strength — α=1 is the classic Laplace, smaller α means less smoothing.
Without smoothing: P(word | class) = count(word, class) / count(all words, class)

With Laplace (α=1): P(word | class) = (count(word, class) + α) / (count(all words, class) + α × |V|)

where |V| = vocabulary size (number of unique words)
Without smoothing
P("crypto" | spam)0/500 = 0.000
Spam score... × 0.000 = 0
EffectSpam score zeroed out
ResultWrong prediction
With Laplace (α=1, |V|=1000)
P("crypto" | spam)(0+1)/(500+1000) = 0.00067
Spam score... × 0.00067 = tiny but nonzero
EffectAll other spam words still count
ResultCorrect prediction ✓

08 · Trade-offs

Naive Bayes punches well above its weight class for text. Knowing its limits helps you choose it wisely.

Very little data neededWorks well even with small datasets
Scales to huge vocabulary10,000+ word features — no problem
Handles missing featuresMissing feature just doesn't multiply in
Probabilistic outputGives confidence, not just a label

Cons

09 · Check your understanding

Quick quiz

Four questions — test yourself on Naive Bayes.

1. Why is Naive Bayes called "naive"?
A. It uses a very simple algorithm that any beginner can understand
B. It performs poorly compared to other classifiers
C. It makes the naive assumption that all features are independent given the class ✓
D. It was invented by a researcher named Naive
The "naive" refers to the conditional independence assumption — that P(f₁,f₂,...|class) = P(f₁|class) × P(f₂|class) × ... This is almost never true (words are correlated), but the simplification makes computation tractable and the results surprisingly good. ✓
2. A word appears 0 times in spam emails during training. Without Laplace smoothing, what happens to the spam score for any email containing that word?
A. It slightly reduces the spam score
B. It is ignored — the word is skipped
C. The entire spam score becomes zero — multiplying by 0 ✓
D. The model falls back to the prior probability
P(unseen word | spam) = 0/n = 0. When you multiply the entire score by 0, the total spam score becomes exactly 0 — regardless of how strongly all other words indicate spam. One unseen word destroys the whole calculation. Laplace smoothing adds α=1 to all counts to prevent this. ✓
3. Which type of Naive Bayes is best suited for classifying emails using word count features?
A. Gaussian Naive Bayes — for continuous distributions
B. Multinomial Naive Bayes — for count data ✓
C. Bernoulli Naive Bayes — for binary presence/absence
D. Any type works equally well for text
Multinomial NB models the distribution of word counts — how many times each word appears. Bernoulli NB uses only presence/absence (0 or 1). Gaussian NB is for continuous features like measurements. For word count or TF-IDF vectors, Multinomial NB is the standard choice. ✓
4. In the formula P(class | features) ∝ P(class) × Π P(fᵢ | class), what does the prior P(class) represent?
A. The probability of the features given this class
B. The base rate — how common this class is in training data ✓
C. The number of training samples in this class
D. The probability that the features appear in the data
The prior P(class) is the base rate — how often this class appears in training data. If 70% of training emails are spam, P(spam)=0.7. It encodes our prior belief before seeing any features. If spam is rare (1%), the prior pulls scores down even for spam-looking words. ✓
