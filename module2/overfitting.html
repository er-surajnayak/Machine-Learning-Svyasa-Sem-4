Overfitting &
Underfitting

The two ways a model can fail — learning too little or memorising too much. Finding the sweet spot between them is the central challenge of every machine learning project.

01 · The core idea

The Goldilocks problem

In machine learning, there's a famous problem with two extremes. Your model can be too simple — it doesn't learn enough from the data. Or it can be too complex — it learns the training data so perfectly that it fails on anything new. The goal is to find the model that is just right.

Analogy — Goldilocks and the Three Bears: Papa Bear's porridge is too hot (too complex — overfit). Mama Bear's is too cold (too simple — underfit). Baby Bear's is just right (good generalisation). Every ML model sits somewhere on this spectrum. Your job is to find Baby Bear's porridge.
Too simple
📉
Underfitting

Fails on training data AND test data. Hasn't learned anything useful.

Just right
✓
Good generalisation

Performs well on both training and new test data. The goal.

Too complex
📈
Overfitting

Nails training data but crashes on test data. Memorised, not learned.

02 · Too simple

Underfitting

An underfit model is too simple to capture the patterns in the data. It performs poorly on both training and test data — it hasn't even learned the training set well. This is called high bias.

Analogy — The student who didn't study: Imagine a student who shows up to an exam having barely read the textbook. They answer every question with vague, generic responses. They score poorly on the exam (test data) AND they'd score poorly if you gave them the practice problems again (training data) — because they never actually learned the material.

Concrete example — fitting a curve

Data follows a curved (quadratic) pattern. An underfit model uses a straight line — it misses the curve entirely, making large errors everywhere.

03 · Too complex

Overfitting

An overfit model is too complex — it learns the training data so precisely that it memorises the noise and outliers, not just the real patterns. It works brilliantly on training data but falls apart on anything new. This is called high variance.

Analogy — The student who memorised every question: A student memorises every single practice question word-for-word, including typos and irrelevant details. On the exact same practice paper: 100%. On the real exam with slightly different phrasing: 40%. They learned the specific questions, not the underlying concepts. That's overfitting.
Symptoms
Training accuracyVery high (near 100%)
Test accuracyMuch lower
Gap between themLarge — the red flag
BiasLow
VarianceHigh
Causes
Model too complexToo many parameters
Too little training dataLearns noise as signal
Training too longMemorises every detail
No regularisationNothing to constrain it
Concrete example — decision tree overfitting

A decision tree with no depth limit keeps splitting until every single leaf has exactly one training sample. It memorises the dataset perfectly.

Training accuracy100% — perfect
Test accuracy60% — terrible
What happenedMemorised noise, not pattern
FixSet max_depth=5, min_samples_leaf=10
04 · The sweet spot

The just-right model

A well-generalised model finds the real patterns in the data — not too simple to miss them, not so complex it starts tracing every wiggle. Its training and test accuracy are both high and close together.

Analogy — The good student: This student understands the concepts deeply. They can solve problems they've never seen before because they grasped the underlying logic — not just the specific examples. They score well on practice AND on the real exam. That's generalisation.

06 · Diagnosis

Detecting overfitting in practice

How do you know if your model is overfitting before you deploy it? There are clear signals to watch for — and standard techniques to catch it during training.

Split data into training set and validation set. Train on training set. Evaluate on both.

Train accuracy ≈ Val accuracyGood fit — proceed
Train accuracy >> Val accuracyOverfitting — reduce complexity
Both lowUnderfitting — increase complexity

Common split ratios: 80/20 or 70/30 train/validation.

k-fold cross-validation is more reliable than a single train/val split. The data is divided into k folds — the model trains on k-1 folds and validates on the remaining one, rotating through all k combinations.

k = 55 training runs, each with 80% train / 20% val
k = 10More reliable, more compute
Mean CV scoreMore robust estimate of true performance
High variance across foldsModel is unstable — likely overfit

Plot training and validation loss epoch by epoch during training. The shape tells the story.

Both decreasing togetherHealthy learning
Train down, val flatStarting to overfit
Train down, val going UPClassic overfitting — stop here
Both flat and highUnderfitting — model too simple
07 · Solutions

Fixing overfitting & underfitting

The fixes are direct opposites. Underfitting needs more complexity or data. Overfitting needs constraints, simplification, or more data.

U
Fixing underfitting — add complexity
›
Use a more complex model — switch from linear to polynomial, or shallow tree to deeper tree

Add more features — feature engineering, polynomial features, interaction terms

Reduce regularisation — lower L1/L2 penalty (lambda), reduce dropout rate

Train longer — more epochs, more iterations, lower learning rate

Check data quality — make sure labels and features are correct
O
Fixing overfitting — add constraints
›
Get more training data — the single best fix; more data makes it harder to memorise

Regularisation (L1 / L2) — penalise large weights, forcing the model to stay simple

Reduce model complexity — fewer layers, shallower trees, fewer features

Dropout — randomly deactivate neurons during training (neural networks)

Early stopping — stop training when validation loss stops improving

Data augmentation — artificially expand training data (flipping, rotating images)

Cross-validation — get a more honest estimate of generalisation performance
Regularisation quick reference
L1 (Lasso)Adds |w| penalty — drives some weights to exactly 0 (feature selection)
L2 (Ridge)Adds w² penalty — shrinks all weights towards 0, none exactly zero
Elastic NetCombination of L1 + L2
Lambda (λ)Regularisation strength — higher = more penalty = simpler model
# Scikit-learn examples from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge, Lasso # Prevent decision tree overfitting tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=5, min_samples_leaf=10) # Ridge (L2) regularisation ridge = Ridge(alpha=1.0) # alpha = lambda # Lasso (L1) regularisation lasso = Lasso(alpha=0.1)

09 · Check your understanding

Quick quiz

Four questions — test yourself on overfitting and underfitting.

1. A model scores 98% on training data and 55% on test data. What is this called?
A. Underfitting — both scores are inconsistent
B. Overfitting — huge gap between train and test ✓
C. Good generalisation — training accuracy is very high
D. Normal variance — all models behave this way
The large gap (98% train vs 55% test) is the classic overfitting signature. The model memorised the training data but failed to learn the underlying pattern. ✓
2. A linear model is used to fit data that follows a complex non-linear pattern. Training accuracy = 61%, Test accuracy = 59%. What is this?
A. Overfitting — test accuracy is lower
B. Underfitting — both accuracies are low and close ✓
C. Good fit — both train and test are consistent
D. Data quality issue — need more features
Both train (61%) and test (59%) are low with a tiny gap — both bad. The model is too simple to capture the real pattern. This is underfitting (high bias). ✓
3. Which of these is the MOST effective fix for overfitting?
A. Train the model for more epochs
B. Add more features to the dataset
C. Get more training data ✓
D. Use a more complex model
More training data is consistently the most effective fix for overfitting. With more data, the model can't memorise specific examples — it's forced to learn the general pattern. Options A and D would make overfitting worse. ✓
4. L2 regularisation (Ridge) works by:
A. Adding more training data to the dataset
B. Removing features with low importance
C. Adding a penalty on large weight values to keep the model simple ✓
D. Randomly dropping neurons during training
L2 (Ridge) adds a w² penalty term to the loss function. This discourages large weights, keeping the model from becoming overly complex. Note: D describes Dropout, which is a different regularisation technique used in neural networks. ✓
