01 · The Problem

02 · Core Idea

What is Hierarchical Clustering?

📌 Definition: Hierarchical clustering builds a tree-like structure called a dendrogram, showing how data points group together at different levels of similarity — without needing K specified upfront.
⬆ Agglomerative — Bottom-Up
⬇ Divisive — Top-Down
🧠 Intuition: Think of organizing a college fest. First you find students with similar interests, then friend circles, then full departments. You build groups gradually — that's hierarchical clustering.

04 · Agglomerative

Bottom-Up Clustering

📌 Definition: Start with every point as its own cluster. Repeatedly merge the two closest clusters until only one cluster remains.
1
Treat every data point as its own individual cluster
2
Compute pairwise distances between all clusters
3
Merge the two closest clusters into one
4
Recompute distances from the new merged cluster to all others
5
Repeat steps 2–4 until all points belong to one cluster
🧠 Visual Intuition: Like water drops joining together. Small drops → bigger drops → one large drop. Each merge is permanent.
Initial:   [IP-A]  [IP-B]  [IP-C]  [IP-D]  [IP-E]
Step 1:    [IP-A, IP-B]    [IP-C]  [IP-D]  [IP-E]
Step 2:    [IP-A, IP-B]    [IP-C, IP-D]    [IP-E]
Step 3:    [IP-A, IP-B, IP-C, IP-D]        [IP-E]
Step 4:    [IP-A, IP-B, IP-C, IP-D, IP-E]
05 · Divisive

Top-Down Clustering

⬆ Agglomerative
  • Starts with N individual clusters
  • Merges the closest two each step
  • Builds tree bottom-up
  • More common, simpler, in scikit-learn
⬇ Divisive
  • Starts with 1 cluster (all points)
  • Splits the most dissimilar each step
  • Builds tree top-down
  • Computationally expensive, rare
🧠 Analogy: Think of a company org chart. You start with the entire company, then divide into departments, then teams, then sub-teams.
Netflix divisive split:

All users → Action lovers / Romance lovers / Anime watchers / Documentary viewers → further sub-splits per category

⚠ Why Agglomerative wins: Divisive requires evaluating all possible splits at each step — computationally explosive. Scikit-learn's AgglomerativeClustering is the standard choice in practice.
06 · Linkage Methods

How Do We Measure Cluster Distance?

⭐ Exam Important

Once we have two clusters, how do we define the distance between them? Your choice dramatically changes the shape of clusters you get.

Single Linkage
D(A, B) = min( d(aᵢ, bⱼ) )
→ Distance = closest pair of points between the two clusters
🧠 Analogy: Two friend groups connect if even ONE person from each group knows each other closely.
⚠ Chaining Effect: Single linkage creates long, stretched chains. One nearby point keeps pulling clusters together even if the overall groups are very different.

Use case: Network analysis, biological sequence grouping, social graphs

Complete Linkage
D(A, B) = max( d(aᵢ, bⱼ) )
→ Distance = farthest pair of points between the two clusters
🧠 Analogy: Two teams can only merge if EVERYONE is reasonably close — even one outlier increases the distance.
✅ Creates compact, tight, well-separated clusters. Preferred when you need clean boundaries.

Use case: Customer segmentation in banking, medical diagnosis grouping

Average Linkage
D(A, B) = (1 / n·m) × Σ d(aᵢ, bⱼ)
→ Distance = average of all pairwise distances between clusters
🧠 Analogy: Instead of closest or farthest, consider the overall average relationship between groups.
✅ Balance between Single and Complete. Generally more stable — often the safest default choice.

Use case: General-purpose clustering, document grouping, gene expression

07 · The Dendrogram

Reading the Tree

📌 Definition: A dendrogram is a tree diagram that records every cluster merge in sequence. The X-axis shows data points; the Y-axis shows the distance at which each merge occurred.
X-axisIndividual data points (leaves of the tree)
Y-axisMerge distance — how dissimilar the clusters were when joined
HeightTaller bar = more different the two clusters were before merging
CuttingDraw a horizontal line to decide the number of clusters
⚠ Most Common Mistake: Students think the Y-axis counts points or shows frequency. It does not. Y-axis = dissimilarity distance at the moment of merge. Always.
💡 How to find the optimal cut: Look for the largest vertical gap. Cut there. That gap is where natural groupings end and forced merging begins.

13 · Evaluation

Advantages & Limitations

✅ Advantages
  • No need to specify K upfront — dendrogram reveals it
  • Full visual hierarchy of relationships
  • Flexible cluster shapes — not limited to spherical
  • Great for exploratory analysis
  • Single run gives ALL possible clusterings at every K
❌ Limitations
  • O(n²) — painful beyond ~50,000 rows
  • Sensitive to outliers — one noisy point distorts merges
  • Early merges are permanent — mistakes cannot be corrected
  • Single linkage chaining can destroy cluster quality
  • No built-in score like WCSS to evaluate quality
14 · Caution

When NOT to Use Hierarchical Clustering

⚠ Hierarchical clustering is powerful, but the wrong tool in several common situations.
1
Dataset has > 50,000 rows
Use K-Means or DBSCAN instead. O(n²) will be too slow and memory-intensive at this scale.
2
You already know K and clusters are spherical
K-Means is faster and simpler. Don't use a sledgehammer for a nail.
3
Real-time or streaming clustering required
Hierarchical clustering recomputes from scratch — no incremental updates. Use online K-Means instead.
4
Data has many outliers
Single linkage will chain outliers into legitimate clusters. Consider DBSCAN, which explicitly handles noise points.
15 · Revision

Short Exam Note

  • Agglomerative = Bottom-Up; Divisive = Top-Down
  • Agglomerative is more commonly used (simpler, in scikit-learn)
  • Single Linkage = minimum pairwise distance (causes chaining)
  • Complete Linkage = maximum pairwise distance (compact clusters)
  • Average Linkage = mean pairwise distance (most stable)
  • Dendrogram Y-axis = merge DISTANCE — not frequency, not count
  • Cut at the largest vertical gap for the natural number of clusters
  • Time complexity ≈ O(n²) — not suitable for very large datasets
  • Does NOT require K to be specified before running
  • Early merges in agglomerative are irreversible
16 · Practice

Quiz Yourself

Score: 0 / 6 correct

Q1. What is the main advantage of hierarchical clustering over K-Means?
Correct! Hierarchical clustering builds a dendrogram and lets you choose K after the fact by cutting the tree — no upfront K needed.
Q2. In agglomerative clustering, what happens at each step?
Correct! Agglomerative = bottom-up. At every step, the two most similar (closest) clusters merge into one.
Q3. Which linkage method uses the MAXIMUM pairwise distance?
Correct! Complete Linkage uses the farthest two points between clusters — this keeps clusters compact and well-separated.
Q4. What does the Y-axis of a dendrogram represent?
Correct! The height (Y-axis) shows dissimilarity at merge time. Taller bars = more different clusters joining.
Q5. Where should you cut a dendrogram to find the optimal clusters?
Correct! The largest vertical gap represents the biggest jump in dissimilarity — cutting there gives the most natural grouping.
Q6. Which linkage method is most likely to suffer from the chaining effect?
Correct! Single linkage connects clusters if even one point is close — this causes long chains where loosely related points keep merging.
